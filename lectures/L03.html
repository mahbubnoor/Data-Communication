Signals arrive changed: three impairments

  • A signal at the end of a medium is never exactly the signal that entered it
  • Attenuation: it gets weaker
  • Distortion: its shape changes
  • Noise: unwanted energy is added
  • The receiver must still recover the original bits

Attenuation: the signal loses energy

  • Attenuation means loss of energy, so a weaker signal
  • Some energy is spent overcoming the resistance of the medium and turns into heat
  • Amplifiers boost an analog signal; repeaters regenerate a digital one

Measuring gain and loss: the decibel

  • The decibel (dB) measures the relative strength of two signals
  • Negative dB means the signal was attenuated; positive dB means it was amplified
  • Use 10 log for power and 20 log for voltage (power is proportional to voltage squared)
  • Logarithms turn huge ratios into small, easy numbers

dB = 10 log₁₀(P₂ / P₁)

dB = 20 log₁₀(V₂ / V₁)

P = power (watts) · V = voltage (volts) · 1 = input, 2 = output

Why engineers love decibels: they add

  • Through several stages, ratios multiply, but decibels simply add
  • Cable losses are quoted per kilometre: dB/km × length = total loss
  • To get back to power: P₂ = P₁ × 10^(dB/10)
  • dBm compares a power with 1 mW: 2 mW = +3 dBm, 0.001 mW = −30 dBm

Distortion: the signal changes shape

  • Distortion means the signal's form or shape changes
  • It affects composite signals, made of several frequencies
  • Each frequency component has its own propagation speed, so they arrive with different delays
  • Components arrive with different phases, so the sum looks different
  • Equalisers at the receiver undo much of the effect

At the sender: f and 3f in step, flat-topped shape

At the receiver: 3f delayed, the shape is bent

Each frequency travels at a slightly different speed, so components drift out of phase.

Noise: unwanted energy added on the way

  • Noise is any unwanted signal added to the one we want
  • Four kinds: thermal, induced, crosstalk and impulse (table)
  • Thermal noise is always present; it can never be removed completely
  • Defences: shielding, twisted pairs, filtering, error-detecting codes

ThermalRandom motion of electrons in any conductorThe hiss on a radio between stations

Signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)

  • SNR = average signal power / average noise power
  • Usually quoted in decibels: SNR(dB) = 10 log₁₀ SNR
  • High SNR: the signal stands clearly above the noise
  • Low SNR: the receiver starts deciding bits wrongly
  • A noiseless channel would have SNR = ∞, an ideal never reached

Worked examples: SNR both ways

  • Problem 1: signal power 10 mW, noise power 1 µW. Find SNR and SNR(dB).

How fast can a channel go? Two limits

  • Three things set the maximum data rate: bandwidth, signal levels and noise
  • Nyquist answers: how fast with L levels, if there were no noise?
  • Shannon answers: what is the absolute ceiling, given the noise?
  • In practice, use Shannon to find the ceiling, then Nyquist to choose the number of levels

Nyquist

Noiseless channel

N = 2 B log₂ L

Depends on bandwidth B and number of signal levels L

Shannon

Noisy channel (every real one)

C = B log₂(1 + SNR)

Depends on bandwidth B and the signal-to-noise ratio

Nyquist bit rate: the noiseless channel

  • BitRate = 2 × B × log₂ L (B in Hz, result in bps)
  • Doubling the levels adds one bit per signal element
  • In theory, more levels always means a higher rate
  • In reality, more levels sit closer together and noise makes them unreadable
Bandwidth BLevels LBits per levelMax bit rate 2B log₂L
3,000 Hz216,000 bps
3,000 Hz4212,000 bps
3,000 Hz16424,000 bps
20 kHz646240 kbps

Need 265 kbps over 20 kHz? log₂L = 265,000 / 40,000 = 6.625, so L ≈ 98.7. Levels must be a power of 2: use 128 levels (280 kbps), or 64 levels and accept 240 kbps.

Shannon capacity: the noisy channel

  • C = B × log₂(1 + SNR), with SNR as a ratio, not in dB
  • C is the theoretical highest data rate with arbitrarily few errors
  • It does not depend on the number of levels: noise decides how many are usable
  • Capacity grows in proportion to bandwidth, but only logarithmically with SNR

Telephone line: B = 3,000 Hz, SNR = 35 dB = 3,162

C = 3,000 × log₂(1 + 3,162)

= 3,000 × 11.62

C ≈ 34,860 bps

Very noisy channel: SNR ≈ 0

C = B log₂(1 + 0) = B × 0 = 0

No data at all, however wide the bandwidth

Worked example: using both limits together

  • Problem: B = 1 MHz and SNR = 63. What rate, and how many signal levels?
  • Shannon gives the ceiling: 6 Mbps
  • Real systems stay below it for safety, so choose 4 Mbps
  • Nyquist then gives the levels needed: L = 4

Step 1 · Shannon ceiling

10⁶ × log₂(1 + 63) = 10⁶ × 6

6 Mbps

Step 2 · choose a safer rate

leave margin below the ceiling

4 Mbps

Step 3 · Nyquist for levels

4 Mbps = 2 × 10⁶ × log₂L → log₂L = 2

L = 4

Shortcut when SNR ≫ 1: C ≈ B × SNR(dB) / 3. For B = 2 MHz, 36 dB: C ≈ 24 Mbps.

Latency: four delays add up

  • Latency = propagation + transmission + queuing + processing
  • Propagation: time for one bit to travel the distance
  • Transmission: time to push all the message's bits onto the link
  • Queuing and processing: time spent inside routers and switches

propagation = distance / speed

Queuing: waiting in router buffers behind other packets. Processing: reading headers and choosing a route.

Worked examples: which delay dominates?

  • Same 12,000 km cable path for both messages: propagation = 50 ms
  • A short email on a fast link: transmission 20 µs, so propagation dominates
  • A large image on a slow link: transmission 40 s, so transmission dominates
  • Rule: small messages care about distance; large ones care about bandwidth

Bandwidth-delay product: bits in flight

  • Bandwidth × delay = the number of bits that fill the link at one moment
  • Picture the link as a pipe: bandwidth is its cross-section, delay is its length
  • Example: 100 Mbps with 20 ms delay holds 2 million bits
  • To use a link fully, a sender must keep this many bits in flight before an answer returns

length of the pipe = delay

height = bandwidth

100 Mbps × 20 ms = 2,000,000 bits

= 250 kB in flight

A sender that waits for a reply after every packet leaves this pipe almost empty (Lecture 12).

Jitter: when delay is uneven

  • Jitter: variation in delay from packet to packet
  • Harmless for downloads, harmful for live voice and video
  • Caused by packets taking different paths and waiting in different queues
  • Fix: a jitter buffer holds packets briefly and plays them at an even pace, adding a little delay

Key takeaways

  • Signals suffer attenuation, distortion and noise on every real channel
  • Decibels: 10 log for power, 20 log for voltage; stages simply add
  • SNR measures quality; convert dB to a ratio before using Shannon
  • Nyquist 2B log₂L (noiseless) and Shannon B log₂(1+SNR) (the true ceiling)
  • Latency = propagation + transmission + queuing + processing; BDP = bits in flight
