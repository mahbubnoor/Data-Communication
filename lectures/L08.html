Why put an analog signal on a carrier?

  • Radio and many cables are band-pass: they carry only a band far from 0 Hz
  • Antennas must be roughly a quarter-wavelength long, impossible for audio frequencies
  • Each sender needs its own band so many can share one medium
  • So the message (low-frequency, baseband) modulates a high-frequency carrier

Antenna for 1 kHz audio sent directly

λ = 3×10⁸ / 10³ = 300 km → λ/4

75 km long!

Antenna for a 100 MHz FM carrier

λ = 3×10⁸ / 10⁸ = 3 m → λ/4

75 cm

Every station's audio uses the same 0 to 15 kHz range. Shifting each to its own carrier frequency lets them share the air without colliding.

Three ways to carry an analog message

  • Amplitude modulation (AM): the carrier's height follows the message
  • Frequency modulation (FM): the carrier's frequency follows the message
  • Phase modulation (PM): the carrier's phase follows the message
  • The carrier's other two properties stay constant in each case

Amplitude modulation (AM)

  • The carrier's amplitude varies with the message; its frequency and phase stay fixed
  • s(t) = Ac [1 + m·cos(2πfm t)] · cos(2πfc t)
  • The outline of the peaks, the envelope, is a copy of the message
  • Implemented by multiplying the carrier by (1 + message)

The modulation index m

  • m measures how deeply the message changes the carrier's amplitude
  • 0 < m < 1: normal AM; the envelope is a faithful copy
  • m = 1: 100% modulation; the envelope just touches zero
  • m > 1: overmodulation; the envelope folds over and the message is distorted

m = 0.5

m = 1 (100%)

m = 1.4: overmodulated

m = (Amax − Amin) / (Amax + Amin)

Example: Amax = 10 V, Amin = 2 V → m = 8 / 12 = 0.67 (67% modulation)

AM spectrum and bandwidth

  • AM shifts the message's spectrum up to the carrier and mirrors it
  • Upper sideband (fc to fc + B) and lower sideband (fc − B to fc)
  • So an AM signal needs twice the message's bandwidth: B(AM) = 2B
  • Both sidebands carry the same information; single-sideband (SSB) sends only one to halve the bandwidth

Worked example: planning the AM band

  • Audio for AM broadcasting is limited to about 5 kHz
  • Each station needs 2 × 5 = 10 kHz (the FCC allocation in Forouzan)
  • Carriers must be at least 10 kHz apart so neighbours do not overlap
  • The AM band, 530 to 1,700 kHz, fits 117 such channels

AM power: most of it is in the carrier

  • The carrier itself carries no information; only the sidebands do
  • Total power Pt = Pc (1 + m²/2)
  • Even at m = 1, the sidebands get only one third of the power
  • This waste is why AM is inefficient, and why SSB and FM were developed

Pt = Pc (1 + m² / 2)

Pc = carrier power · the extra Pc·m²/2 is shared equally by the two sidebands

Pc = 100 W, m = 0.8

100 × (1 + 0.64/2) = 100 × 1.32

Frequency modulation (FM)

  • The carrier's frequency varies with the message's value; amplitude and phase stay fixed
  • The frequency swings by up to Δf (the peak deviation) either side of fc
  • The modulation index is β = Δf / fm
  • Implemented with a voltage-controlled oscillator (VCO): the message voltage sets the frequency

f(t) = fc + Δf · m(t)

Δf = peak frequency deviation · β = Δf / fm = modulation index

FM spectrum and bandwidth: Carson's rule

  • FM produces many sidebands at fc ± fm, ± 2fm, …
  • Their strengths follow Bessel functions of β; beyond about β + 1 they become tiny
  • Carson's rule: B(FM) ≈ 2(1 + β)B
  • Larger β: better noise immunity, but more bandwidth

β = 4: lines at fc ± n·fm; shaded = Carson's bandwidth

B(FM) = 2(1 + β)B = 2(Δf + B)

Carson's rule: contains about 98% of the power. Forouzan uses β = 4 for broadcasting.

Worked example: planning the FM band

  • FM stereo audio extends to about 15 kHz
  • With β = 4: B = 2(1 + 4) × 15 = 150 kHz; the allocation is 200 kHz per station
  • The FM band 88 to 108 MHz holds 20 / 0.2 = 100 channels
  • Only alternate channels are used in one area, to avoid interference: about 50

Why FM sounds cleaner than AM

  • Most noise (thermal, impulse, lightning) changes the signal's amplitude
  • AM carries the message in the amplitude, so the noise goes straight into the audio
  • FM carries it in the frequency, so a limiter can strip amplitude changes away
  • The price: much more bandwidth (200 kHz vs 10 kHz)

AM + amplitude noise · the noise changes the envelope itself

FM + amplitude noise · the information is in the zero crossings

An FM receiver first clips the signal to a constant height (a limiter), discarding amplitude noise, then reads the frequency.

Phase modulation (PM)

  • The carrier's phase varies with the message's value; amplitude stays fixed
  • s(t) = Ac cos(2πfc t + kp·m(t))
  • PM of a message = FM of its derivative: the frequency shifts where the message changes
  • Same bandwidth formula as FM, but a smaller β in practice

Message · a smoothed square wave

FM · frequency follows the message's value

PM · phase follows the value; frequency changes only where the message changes

B(PM) = 2(1 + β)B, with β ≈ 1 (narrowband) to 3 (wideband): smaller than FM's

Getting the message back: demodulation

  • The receiver reverses the modulation to recover the message
  • AM: a diode and an RC filter follow the envelope; the simplest radio receiver
  • FM: limit the amplitude, then convert frequency changes into voltage
  • Real radios first shift the chosen station to a fixed intermediate frequency (superheterodyne)

AM: envelope detector

Diode: keep the top half

RC filter: follow the peaks

Audio out

FM: limiter, then discriminator

Clip to constant height, then turn frequency changes into voltage (a frequency-to-voltage converter or a phase-locked loop).

Key takeaways

  • Analog messages ride on a carrier for antenna size, band-pass media and sharing
  • AM: envelope follows the message; m = (Amax − Amin)/(Amax + Amin); B = 2B; Pt = Pc(1 + m²/2)
  • FM: frequency follows the message; β = Δf/fm; Carson B = 2(1 + β)B
  • PM: phase follows the message; same formula with smaller β; FM of the derivative
  • Broadcast plans: AM 10 kHz per station (9 kHz in Asia); FM 200 kHz per station
