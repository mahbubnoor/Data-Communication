What is data communication?

  • Data communication: the exchange of data between two devices over a transmission medium (wire, fibre or air)
  • Tele means far: telecommunication is communication at a distance
  • Data: information represented in a form agreed on by sender and receiver
  • A system is effective only if it meets four characteristics (right)

Delivery

Data reaches the correct destination, and only that destination

Accuracy

Data arrives unaltered; corrupted data is unusable

Timeliness

Data arrives in time; late audio or video is useless

Jitter

Delay between packets stays even, not uneven

Five components of a communication system

  • Message: the information sent (text, numbers, images, audio, video)
  • Sender and receiver: the devices at each end
  • Transmission medium: the physical path, such as twisted pair, fibre or radio waves
  • Protocol: the rules both sides agree on; without it, devices are connected but cannot communicate

Representing data as bits

  • All data is ultimately a pattern of bits (0s and 1s)
  • Text: each character has a code; ASCII for English, Unicode for every script, including Bengali
  • Numbers: converted directly to binary
  • Images: a grid of pixels; each pixel stored as bits (1 bit black-and-white, 24 bits full colour)
  • Audio and video: continuous signals that must be sampled and digitised (Lecture 5)

Data flow: simplex, half-duplex, full-duplex

  • Simplex: one direction only; one device always sends, the other only receives
  • Half-duplex: both directions, but one at a time; the sender gets the full channel
  • Full-duplex: both directions simultaneously; the channel capacity is shared between the two directions
  • Choice depends on the application: a sensor reading needs simplex, a conversation needs duplex

Networks and the three criteria they must meet

  • Network: a set of devices (nodes) connected by communication links
  • Performance: measured mainly by throughput and delay (latency)
  • Reliability: how often it fails, how long recovery takes, how robust it is in a disaster
  • Security: protecting data from unauthorised access, damage and loss

Type of connection: point-to-point and multipoint

  • Link: the communication pathway that carries data between devices
  • Point-to-point: a dedicated link between exactly two devices; all its capacity serves that pair
  • Multipoint (multidrop): more than two devices share one link
  • Sharing happens in space (at the same time) or in time (taking turns)

Physical topology: four basic layouts

  • Topology: the geometric arrangement of all links and nodes
  • Mesh: every device has a dedicated link to every other device
  • Star: every device links only to a central hub or switch
  • Bus: all devices tap into one long backbone cable
  • Ring: each device links to its two neighbours; signals travel around the loop

Worked example: cabling an office of 10 computers

  • Problem: connect n = 10 computers in a full mesh. How many links? How many ports per computer?
  • Each computer links to the other n − 1 = 9, so it needs 9 ports
  • Each duplex link joins two devices, so count it once: n(n − 1)/2 = 10 × 9 / 2 = 45 links
  • Same office as a star: 10 links, one port per computer, plus a 10-port switch

Network types by scale: LAN, MAN, WAN

  • LAN: privately owned; links devices in one office, building or campus
  • MAN: covers a town or city
  • WAN: spans a country, continent or the world; usually run by a carrier
  • WANs are point-to-point (one link joining two devices) or switched (many links joined by switches)
  • Internetwork (an internet): two or more networks connected together

Protocols: the rules of communication

  • A protocol defines what is communicated, how it is communicated, and when
  • Connected devices without a shared protocol are like two people with no common language
  • Every protocol has three key elements: syntax, semantics and timing
  • Protocols you already use: HTTP, TCP, IP, Ethernet, Wi-Fi (IEEE 802.11)

Syntax

≈ grammar

The structure or format of the data

Ethernet: first 6 bytes = destination address, next 6 = source

Semantics

≈ vocabulary

The meaning of each section of bits

Does this field mean “data”, “acknowledge” or “resend”?

Timing

≈ turn-taking

When data is sent and how fast

A 100 Mbps sender can overwhelm a 1 Mbps receiver

Standards: why any device works with any other

  • Standards guarantee interoperability across manufacturers and countries
  • De facto: adopted through widespread use, without formal approval
  • De jure: formally approved by a recognised standards body
  • Internet standards start as Internet Drafts and are published as RFCs (Requests for Comments)

Why layers? The postal letter analogy

  • Complex communication is split into layers; each does one job and serves the layer above
  • Each layer on the sender has a matching peer layer on the receiver (dashed lines)
  • Layers are independent: change the carrier from truck to plane and the letter is unchanged
  • Computer networks are organised the same way, as the next slide shows

OSI and TCP/IP: two layered models

  • Application: services for the user, such as web, email and file transfer

Encapsulation: each layer adds its own header

  • Going down, each layer wraps the data from above with its own header (H)
  • The data link layer also adds a trailer (T), used for error detection (Lecture 11)
  • The physical layer sends the whole frame as a stream of bits
  • Going up, each layer removes its header: decapsulation
  • Unit names: message → segment → datagram → frame → bits

Key takeaways

  • Data communication is judged by delivery, accuracy, timeliness and jitter
  • Every system has five components: message, sender, receiver, medium and protocol
  • Data flows as simplex, half-duplex or full-duplex
  • Topology sets cost and robustness: a full mesh needs n(n−1)/2 links, a star needs n
  • Layered models (OSI, TCP/IP) split the work; this course lives in the bottom two layers
