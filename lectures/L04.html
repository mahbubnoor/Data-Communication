Two families of transmission media

  • A transmission medium carries signals from sender to receiver; it sits below the physical layer
  • Guided media confine the signal inside a physical conductor or fibre
  • Unguided media send electromagnetic waves through air, water or vacuum
  • Copper carries electrical signals; fibre carries light; wireless carries radio, microwave or infrared

Twisted-pair cable

  • Two insulated copper conductors, usually about 1 mm thick, twisted together
  • One wire carries the signal; the other is the reference (return path)
  • The receiver uses the difference between the two wires
  • Cheapest and most common medium: telephone lines and Ethernet LANs

One pair: two insulated copper wires twisted together

An Ethernet cable holds four such pairs, each twisted at a slightly different rate.

Why twisting cancels noise

  • Untwisted, the wire nearer a noise source picks up more noise than the far one
  • Twisting swaps which wire is nearer, many times per metre, so both pick up equal noise
  • The receiver subtracts: (s + n) − (−s + n) = 2s
  • This also cuts crosstalk from neighbouring pairs

Wire A: +signal + noise

Wire B: −signal + the same noise

Receiver: A − B = 2 × signal; the noise cancels

Unshielded and shielded twisted pair

  • UTP: no metal shield; relies on twisting alone
  • STP: adds a metal foil or braided shield that blocks outside interference
  • Both end in the 8-pin RJ-45 connector used for Ethernet
  • The shield must be grounded properly, or it can make noise worse

UTP

Unshielded: four pairs inside a plastic jacket

Cheap, thin, flexible: almost all office and home Ethernet

STP

Shielded: a metal foil or braid around the pairs

Better noise immunity, but bulkier and costlier: factories, data centres

Worked example: how far can copper go?

  • Problem: a receiver needs at least 1% of the transmitted power
  • Cat 6 loses roughly 20 dB per 100 m at 100 MHz
  • Allowed loss: 10 log₁₀(0.01) = −20 dB
  • Length = 20 dB ÷ 0.2 dB/m = 100 m, matching the Ethernet rule

Coaxial cable

  • A central copper conductor surrounded by an insulator, a metal shield and a jacket
  • The shield fully surrounds the signal, so coax handles much higher frequencies than twisted pair
  • Rated by RG numbers; connectors include BNC and the screw-on F-type
  • Today: cable TV, cable broadband, satellite dishes, CCTV

Bending light: refraction and the critical angle

  • Light changes direction when it passes between materials of different refractive index n
  • Going from denser (higher n) to less dense (lower n), it bends away from the normal
  • At the critical angle θc the refracted ray skims along the boundary
  • Above θc, all the light reflects back: total internal reflection

Below θc: refracts out

At θc: skims the boundary

Above θc: reflects back

n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂

sin θc = n₂ / n₁

Angles are measured from the normal (dashed line). Light travels from n₁ (denser, bottom) towards n₂.

Worked example: a fibre's critical angle

  • Problem: core index n₁ = 1.48, cladding index n₂ = 1.46
  • Rays must hit the core–cladding boundary at more than θc ≈ 80.6° from the normal
  • So light must travel almost parallel to the fibre axis to stay trapped
  • Light entering within about ±14° of the axis is guided; steeper light escapes

Critical angle

sin θc = 1.46 / 1.48 = 0.9865

θc ≈ 80.6°

Numerical aperture

NA = √(n₁² − n₂²) = √(2.1904 − 2.1316)

NA ≈ 0.24

Acceptance angle (in air)

sin θa = NA → θa = arcsin 0.24

≈ ±14°

Optical fibre: light trapped in glass

  • A thin glass or plastic core surrounded by cladding of slightly lower refractive index
  • Light hits the boundary above the critical angle and reflects, again and again
  • A protective buffer and jacket surround the cladding
  • Data is sent as pulses of light, usually from a laser or LED; a photodiode detects them
  • Light travels one way, so a link normally uses a pair of fibres

Propagation modes: how many paths?

  • Multimode step-index: many paths of different lengths; pulses spread the most
  • Multimode graded-index: index falls gradually from the centre, so paths re-align; less spreading
  • Single-mode: a core so narrow that light takes essentially one path; almost no modal spreading
  • Less spreading means pulses can be closer together: higher data rates over longer distances

Why fibre carries the world's data

  • Fibre forms the backbone of the Internet, mobile networks and undersea cables
  • Common connectors: SC (push-pull), ST (twist-lock) and the small LC
  • Fibre-to-the-home (FTTH) now brings fibre into many homes and offices
  • Copper remains for short, cheap connections to individual devices

Advantages

Enormous bandwidth: terabits per second per fibre with many wavelengths

Very low loss: about 0.2 dB/km, so tens of km between amplifiers

Immune to electromagnetic noise and lightning

Light, thin and hard to tap without detection

Disadvantages

Installation and splicing need skilled work and special tools

Light travels one way: links need fibre pairs

Transceivers and connectors cost more than copper's

Fragile if bent too tightly

Worked example: copper versus fibre

  • Copper (Cat 6 at 100 MHz): about 0.2 dB per metre
  • Single-mode fibre at 1550 nm: about 0.2 dB per kilometre
  • Same 20 dB loss: 100 m of copper, or 100 km of fibre
  • Fibre goes about 1,000 times further for the same loss

Three ways radio waves travel

  • Ground propagation: low frequencies hug the Earth's surface, reaching far at low data rates
  • Sky propagation: waves bounce off the ionosphere, reaching other continents
  • Line-of-sight propagation: high frequencies travel straight; antennas must see each other
  • The frequency decides which method dominates

━ Ground wave (below 2 MHz)

━ Sky wave (2–30 MHz)

━ Line of sight (above 30 MHz)

Worked example: why towers are tall

  • Line-of-sight antennas must see each other over the curve of the Earth
  • The maximum distance grows with the square root of antenna height
  • Doubling the distance needs four times the height
  • In practice, the atmosphere bends waves slightly, extending range by about 15%

d ≈ 3.57 (√h₁ + √h₂) km

h₁, h₂ = antenna heights in metres; ignores atmospheric bending

Two 100 m towers

≈ 71 km

Phone (1.5 m) to a 30 m mobile tower

3.57 × (√1.5 + √30) = 3.57 × 6.70

≈ 24 km

Radio waves, microwaves and infrared

Radio waves

3 kHz – 1 GHz

Direction: omnidirectional: spread in all directions

Walls: pass through walls

Uses: AM and FM radio, TV broadcast, maritime radio

Microwaves

1 – 300 GHz

Direction: unidirectional with dish or horn antennas; Wi-Fi and phones use wide-beam antennas

Walls: higher frequencies are blocked more easily

Uses: mobile networks, Wi-Fi, satellite, point-to-point links

Infrared

300 GHz – 400 THz

Direction: short-range line of sight

Walls: blocked by walls

Uses: TV remotes, short-range device links

Omnidirectional antennas suit broadcasting to many receivers; directional dishes concentrate energy towards one receiver, increasing range and reducing interference.

Key takeaways

  • Guided media (twisted pair, coax, fibre) confine the signal; unguided media radiate it
  • Twisting plus differential signalling cancels noise; shields block it
  • Fibre guides light by total internal reflection: sin θc = n₂ / n₁
  • Single-mode fibre keeps pulses sharp; it carries data furthest and fastest
  • Radio propagation: ground, sky or line of sight, decided by frequency
