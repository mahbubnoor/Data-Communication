Radio frequencies and wavelength

  • Wireless systems use radio waves and microwaves (Lecture 4)
  • Higher frequency → shorter wavelength → smaller antennas
  • Higher frequency also → more loss and worse penetration of walls
  • ISM bands (902–928 MHz, 2.400–2.4835 GHz, 5.725–5.850 GHz) need no licence
  • Cellular bands are licensed to operators

Free-space path loss

  • Power spreads over a sphere: received power falls as 1/d²
  • In dB, loss grows by 6 dB for every doubling of distance
  • Higher frequency: smaller receiving antenna, so more loss (+6 dB per doubling of f)
  • Worked: Wi-Fi at 2.4 GHz over 100 m loses 80 dB (a factor of 108)
  • Real buildings lose more: walls, bodies, furniture

A link budget

  • Add up gains and losses along the link, all in dB
  • Received power must stay above the receiver's sensitivity
  • The surplus is the fade margin: room for walls, rain, multipath
  • −56 dBm is about 2.5 × 10−9 W: tiny, but plenty
  • Free space would reach ~2 km; walls cut indoor Wi-Fi to tens of metres

Pr = Pt + Gt + Gr − L

all in dB / dBm; dBm = dB relative to 1 mW (Lecture 3)

Multipath and fading

  • Reflections give the receiver several copies of the signal
  • Copies in phase add; half a cycle apart they cancel: a fade
  • Moving a few centimetres (λ/2) can change strong to weak
  • Long echo delays smear symbols into each other: inter-symbol interference
  • Fading is why wireless links need margin, error control and clever modulation

How fast can a wireless link go?

  • Bandwidth sets the symbol rate; SNR sets the bits per symbol
  • Shannon: C = B log₂(1 + SNR), with SNR as a ratio, not dB
  • Worked: 20 MHz at 25 dB SNR → at most about 166 Mbps
  • Radios adapt: dense QAM near the AP, robust BPSK/QPSK at the edge
  • So the same Wi-Fi shows different speeds in different rooms

C = B log₂(1 + SNR)

Shannon capacity (Lecture 3): the upper limit for any modulation

IEEE 802.11 architecture

  • BSS: the basic building block; ad hoc (no AP) or infrastructure (with an AP)
  • Access point: joins the wireless and wired worlds
  • ESS: several BSSs linked by a distribution system
  • Stations may roam between BSSs of one ESS
  • Like cellular: BSS ≈ cell, AP ≈ base station

802.11 MAC: DCF, PCF and the frame

  • DCF: CSMA/CA with RTS/CTS and ACKs (Lecture 13)
  • PCF: optional polling by the AP for time-sensitive data
  • When both run, a repetition interval mixes a contention-free and a contention period
  • The Duration field sets other stations' NAV
  • Up to four addresses; FCS is a CRC-32 (Lecture 11)

DCF

Distributed coordination: CSMA/CA, DIFS, RTS/CTS, NAV, ACK (Lecture 13). Mandatory; contention service.

PCF

Point coordination: the AP polls stations; optional, for time-sensitive traffic; contention-free service.

Bluetooth: piconets and scatternets

  • A short-range (~10 m) ad hoc network of gadgets (IEEE 802.15.1)
  • Piconet: one primary, up to 7 active secondaries (more may be parked)
  • Secondaries follow the primary's clock and hopping sequence
  • Scatternet: a station in two piconets links them
  • Radio: FHSS over 79 channels, 1600 hops/s; time slots of 625 µs (TDD)

Cellular networks: frequency reuse

  • The area is divided into cells, each with a base station (BS)
  • Base stations connect to a mobile switching centre (MSC) and the phone network
  • Neighbouring cells use different frequency groups
  • A reuse pattern of N cells repeats; red cells share group 1
  • Low transmit power keeps each cell's signal local

Reuse factor

N = i² + ij + j², i = 2, j = 1

How many calls fit in a cell?

  • Channels per cell = channels available ÷ N
  • AMPS: 832 channels, two operators, control channels, N = 7 → 56 calls per cell
  • GSM: 124 carriers × 8 slots = 992; N can be as low as 3
  • GSM: digital voice, error correction → tolerates more interference
  • Smaller cells (micro/pico) reuse frequencies more often → more capacity

Calls, handoff and roaming

  • The MSC sets up calls and assigns channels
  • Incoming call: the MSC pages cells to find the phone
  • Moving between cells: the call is handed off to a new BS
  • Hard handoff: break before make; soft: make before break
  • Roaming: service from another provider's network
EventWhat happens
Making a callphone scans for the strongest setup channel → BS → MSC → called party; MSC assigns a voice channel
ReceivingMSC pages all cells to find the phone, then rings it and assigns a channel
Hard handoffbreak with the old BS, then connect to the new one (1G, 2G)
Soft handofftalk to old and new BS at once, then drop the old (CDMA, 3G)
Roaminganother operator serves you under an agreement

Satellites and Kepler's law

  • A satellite relays signals between distant points on Earth
  • Kepler: period = C × distance1.5, C ≈ 1/100 (s, km from Earth's centre)
  • At 35,786 km the period is 24 h: geostationary (GEO)
  • Orbits: equatorial, inclined or polar
  • Footprint: the area a satellite's signal covers

Key takeaways

  • Radio loses power with distance (≈ 6 dB per doubling) and fades with multipath
  • Link budget: Pr = Pt + Gt + Gr − L; keep a margin above sensitivity
  • 802.11: BSS/ESS, DCF (CSMA/CA) and PCF, four addresses; Bluetooth piconets
  • Cellular: frequency reuse N, D = R√(3N), handoff; 1G → 5G
  • Satellites: Kepler's law; GEO 24 h and ~0.24 s delay; LEO low delay, many satellites
